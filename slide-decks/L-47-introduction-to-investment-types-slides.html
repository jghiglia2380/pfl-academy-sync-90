Key Terms

4 / 20

Stock (Equity)

Ownership shares in a company that entitle you to a portion of the company's assets and earnings

Example: Buying Apple stock makes you a part-owner of Apple Inc.

Bond (Fixed Income)

A loan you make to a government or corporation that pays regular interest and returns principal at maturity

Example: A 10-year Treasury bond paying 4% annually on $10,000

Mutual Fund

An investment vehicle that pools money from many investors to purchase a diversified portfolio of securities

Example: Fidelity 500 Fund owns stocks in 500 large companies

Exchange-Traded Fund (ETF)

Similar to a mutual fund but trades on stock exchanges like individual stocks with typically lower fees

Example: VOO tracks S&P 500 with only 0.03% annual fee

Key Terms (Continued)

5 / 20

Dividend

A portion of a company's earnings distributed to shareholders, usually quarterly

Example: Receive $0.25 per share every quarter from your stock holdings

Capital Gains

The profit realized when you sell an investment for more than you paid for it

Example: Buy stock at $50, sell at $75 = $25 capital gain per share

Risk Tolerance

Your ability and willingness to withstand losses in exchange for potentially higher returns

Example: High risk tolerance allows more stocks; low tolerance prefers bonds

Diversification

Spreading investments across different asset types to reduce risk

Example: "Don't put all your eggs in one basket" - own stocks AND bonds

Understanding Stocks

6 / 20

Ownership in Companies

When you buy stock, you become a part-owner of the business with two sources of potential returns:

  • Capital appreciation - stock price increases over time
  • Dividends - quarterly payments from company profits
  • Stock values fluctuate based on company performance, economic conditions, and investor sentiment
  • Recommended for long-term goals where you have time to ride out volatility

Stocks vs. Bonds: Core Differences

7 / 20

📈 Stocks (Ownership)

  • ● You're an owner - share in company success
  • ● Potential for high returns (historically ~10%/year)
  • ● Higher volatility - prices swing widely
  • ● May pay dividends (not guaranteed)
  • ● No maturity date - hold as long as you want
  • ● Best for long-term growth goals (10+ years)

📊 Bonds (Lending)

  • ● You're a lender - earn fixed interest
  • ● More predictable returns (typically 3-5%)
  • ● Lower volatility - more stable prices
  • ● Regular interest payments (guaranteed)
  • ● Returns principal at specific maturity date
  • ● Best for stability and near-term goals

Understanding Bonds

8 / 20

Lending Money for Interest

When you buy a bond, you lend money to a government or corporation:

  • Treasury bonds - issued by federal government, virtually risk-free
  • Municipal bonds - issued by states/cities, often tax-free
  • Corporate bonds - issued by companies, higher rates but more risk
  • Credit risk - borrower may not repay (bond ratings help assess)
  • Interest rate risk - existing bonds lose value when rates rise

Understanding Mutual Funds

9 / 20

Pooled Investments for Instant Diversification

Mutual funds pool money from thousands of investors to create diversified portfolios managed by professionals. This solves a major problem: how can regular people diversify without millions of dollars?


Types of Mutual Funds:

• Stock funds - invest in stocks of large, small, or international companies

• Bond funds - invest in government or corporate bonds

• Balanced funds - own both stocks and bonds

• Target-date funds - automatically adjust based on retirement date


Key consideration: Expense ratios (annual fees) compound over time - a 1% fee difference can cost you 25% of your wealth over 30 years!

How Pooling Works

10,000 investors × $5,000 each = $50 million pool to buy hundreds of different securities

Fee Impact Example

$10,000 at 8% for 30 years: 0.05% fee = $99,580 vs 1% fee = $74,093

The Difference

$25,487 lost to fees over 30 years on just $10,000!

Understanding ETFs

10 / 20

The Best of Both Worlds

ETFs combine features of mutual funds and stocks:

  • Diversification like mutual funds - own hundreds of securities
  • Trade like stocks - buy/sell any time market is open
  • Much lower fees - often 0.03% to 0.25% annually
  • More tax-efficient than mutual funds
  • Daily transparency - see exactly what you own
  • Low minimum investment - just buy one share

Popular example: VOO (Vanguard S&P 500 ETF) - own 500 of America's largest companies for 0.03% annual fee

The Power of Starting Early

12 / 20

Time Is Your Greatest Asset

Compound returns make starting early incredibly valuable:

  • Early investments have the longest time to compound
  • Even small amounts grow substantially over decades
  • You don't need to be an expert - simple strategies work
  • Waiting for the "perfect time" often means never starting
  • Automated monthly contributions build wealth consistently

Key insight: It's not about timing the market - it's about time IN the market.

Real-World Example: Priya's Index Fund Approach

13 / 20

Simple Strategy, Powerful Results

Priya, 24, landed her first job earning $45,000 annually. Feeling overwhelmed by investment choices, she researched simple strategies.

  • Invested 15% of income ($563/month)
  • Split between two low-cost ETFs: 80% stocks, 20% bonds
  • Both ETFs charged under 0.05% in fees
  • Automated monthly contributions
  • Experienced both +25% gains and -15% losses
  • Never panicked - kept contributing steadily

Result After 6 Years

Built portfolio worth over $52,000 despite market volatility through discipline and low costs

Success Factors

Simplicity, consistency, low fees, and emotional discipline - no expertise required

Real-World Example: Marcus's Stock-Picking Lessons

14 / 20

Learning Through Experience

Marcus, 26, was convinced he could beat the market by picking individual stocks. He researched companies intensively.

  • Built portfolio of 8 carefully selected stocks
  • Checked prices obsessively every day
  • Panic-sold biotech stock after 40% loss
  • Held declining retail stock "hoping it would recover"
  • Made emotional decisions based on daily swings
  • After 2 years: underperformed index fund by 8%

Costly Mistakes

$15,000 became $16,200 vs. $18,600 if invested in simple index fund - a $2,400 lesson

Valuable Lesson Learned

Shifted to index funds for core portfolio, keeps 10% for individual stocks to satisfy interest
