Key Terms

4 / 20

Probability

The likelihood of a specific event occurring, expressed as a number between 0 and 1 (or 0% to 100%)
Example: Probability of rolling a 6 on a die = 1/6 = 0.167 = 16.7%

Odds

The ratio between the probability of an event occurring versus not occurring
Example: Odds of rolling a 6 are 1:5 (one way to win, five ways to lose)

House Edge

The mathematical advantage a gambling game has over players, ensuring profit for the organizer
Example: American roulette has a 5.26% house edge

Expected Value

The average amount a player can expect to win or lose per bet over many repetitions
Example: A negative expected value means you'll lose money over time

Key Terms

5 / 20

Independent Events

Events where the outcome of one does not influence the outcome of another
Example: Each roulette spin or coin flip is independent—past results don't affect future ones

Dependent Events

Events where the outcome of one event affects the probability of the next
Example: Drawing cards without replacement changes odds for subsequent draws

Gambler's Fallacy

The mistaken belief that past events influence future outcomes in independent events
Example: Thinking red is "due" after black appears 10 times in roulette

Variance

A measure of how much outcomes can differ from the expected value
Example: High variance means short-term results may differ greatly from expectations

Understanding Probability

6 / 20

The Probability Formula

Probability = Favorable Outcomes ÷ Total Possible Outcomes

Example Calculations:

  • Coin flip (heads): 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5 = 50%
  • Rolling a 6 on a die: 1 ÷ 6 = 0.167 ≈ 16.7%
  • Drawing an ace: 4 ÷ 52 = 0.077 ≈ 7.7%

Independent vs. Dependent Events

7 / 20

🎰 Independent Events

Past outcomes don't affect future results

  • Each coin flip is independent
  • Every roulette spin is fresh
  • Slot machine pulls are unrelated
  • Dice rolls don't affect each other

🃏 Dependent Events

Past outcomes change future probabilities

  • Drawing cards without replacement
  • Lottery ball drawings
  • Deck composition changes
  • Probability shifts after each event

📊 Multiple Independent Events

Multiply probabilities together:

  • Heads twice in a row: ½ × ½ = ¼ = 25%
  • Two sixes on two dice: 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36

📊 Multiple Dependent Events

Probability changes after each event:

  • Two aces from deck: 4/52 × 3/51 = 0.45%
  • Second draw uses new total (51 cards)

The House Edge

8 / 20

Why The House Always Wins

The house edge is the mathematical advantage that ensures gambling operators make a profit over time, regardless of short-term outcomes.

The house edge represents the percentage of each bet the house expects to keep on average. Different games have different advantages:

Game House Edge
Blackjack (basic strategy) 0.5% - 2%
Craps 1.4% - 5%
European Roulette 2.7%
American Roulette 5.26%
Slot Machines 2% - 15%
Keno 25% - 30%
Lottery 40% - 50%

Expected Value: The Math of Gambling

9 / 20

What You Can Expect to Win or Lose

EV = (Probability of Winning × Amount Won) - (Probability of Losing × Amount Lost)

Example: Roulette Bet on Red ($10)

Win probability: 18/38 (47.4%)
EV = (0.474 × $10) - (0.526 × $10) = -$0.52
Lose ~52 cents per $10 bet on average

Example: Lottery Ticket ($2)

Win probability: ~1 in 300,000,000
EV ≈ ($0.30) - ($2) = -$1.70
Lose ~$1.70 per ticket on average

A negative expected value means players lose money over time. The more you play, the more you lose.

Common Gambling Fallacies

10 / 20

Mistaken Beliefs About Gambling

The Gambler's Fallacy

The mistaken belief that if an event happens frequently, it will happen less frequently in the future. Reality: Each independent event has the same probability.

The Hot Hand Fallacy

Believing that a person who has experienced success will continue to succeed. Reality: Past wins don't affect future outcomes in games of chance.

The Monte Carlo Fallacy

In 1913, gamblers lost millions betting against black when it came up 26 times in a row—thinking red was "due." Reality: Each spin was still 50/50.

The Truth

In truly independent events, each outcome is unaffected by previous results. The roulette wheel has no memory, the dice don't know what they rolled before.

Financial Risks of Gambling

11 / 20

⚠️ Short-Term Risks

  • Loss of money needed for essentials
  • Emotional decisions to "chase losses"
  • Using credit cards to gamble
  • Borrowing money to continue playing

⚠️ Long-Term Risks

  • Accumulating losses due to negative EV
  • Development of problematic gambling
  • Opportunity cost vs. saving/investing
  • Financial instability and debt

🚩 Warning Signs of Problematic Gambling

  • Using gambling to escape problems
  • Lying about gambling activity
  • Borrowing money to gamble
  • Failed attempts to cut back
  • Needing larger amounts for excitement
  • Gambling with money you can't afford

Responsible Gambling Practices

12 / 20

✅ Set Limits

  • Decide budget in advance and stick to it
  • Set a time limit before you start
  • Only gamble with money you can afford to lose
  • Never use credit cards to gamble

✅ Maintain Perspective

  • Expect to lose (it's the cost of entertainment)
  • Never chase losses
  • Take regular breaks
  • Don't gamble when upset or stressed

💡 View Gambling as Entertainment, Not Income

The most financially healthy approach to gambling is treating it as an entertainment expense—like movies or dining out. Set a fixed amount you're willing to spend, enjoy the experience, and expect that money to be gone. This mindset helps prevent financial harm while still allowing for recreation.

Real-World Example: The Lottery Pool

14 / 20

Understanding the Math

Coworkers formed a lottery pool, contributing $5 weekly. After weeks without winning, some wanted to increase contributions, believing they were "due for a win."

Carlos explained the math:

  • • Each drawing is independent
  • • Previous draws don't affect future odds
  • • Jackpot odds: ~1 in 300,000,000
  • • Increasing spending doesn't improve odds
  • • Expected value is -$1.70 per $2 ticket

⚠️ The Fallacy

"We're due for a win" is the gambler's fallacy—past losses don't increase future chances

✓ Healthy Perspective

Some members continued with realistic expectations; others redirected money to investing

💡 The Math

$5/week = $260/year with negative expected value vs. $260/year invested at 7% = $14,000+ in 20 years
