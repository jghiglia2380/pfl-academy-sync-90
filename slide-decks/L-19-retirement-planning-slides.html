Definition

The process where returns on your money generate their own returns—earning interest on interest.

The Magic

$10,000 at 7% for 40 years = $150,000 (without adding any more money!)

Opportunity Cost
Definition

What you give up when making one financial choice over another—the road not taken.

Example

Spending $200 on sneakers means losing $200 that could have grown in your 401(k)

Saving vs. Investing for Retirement

7 / 20

🏦 Saving

  • Short-term needs (1-3 years)
  • Lower risk to principal
  • Lower returns (1-5%)
  • High liquidity (easy access)
  • Goal: Preserve capital
  • Examples: Savings accounts, CDs
VS

📈 Investing

  • Long-term goals (3+ years)
  • Higher risk (varies by type)
  • Potential for higher returns (7-10%)
  • Lower liquidity (tied up longer)
  • Goal: Grow capital
  • Examples: Stocks, bonds, mutual funds

Retirement Account Options

8 / 20

Your Retirement Toolkit

You have several tax-advantaged options for retirement savings. Understanding each helps you maximize your strategy.

  • 401(k): Employer-sponsored. Pre-tax contributions, employer match (FREE money!), $23,000 limit in 2024
  • Traditional IRA: Tax-deductible contributions NOW, pay taxes when you withdraw in retirement
  • Roth IRA: Pay taxes NOW, withdrawals in retirement are completely TAX-FREE (great for young people)
  • Social Security: Government program, funded by payroll taxes. Provides baseline income in retirement

💡 Pro tip: If your employer offers a 401(k) match, ALWAYS contribute enough to get the full match—it's free money!

Traditional vs. Roth: When to Use Each

9 / 20

Traditional (401k or IRA)

How it works: Contribute pre-tax dollars now, pay taxes when you withdraw in retirement.

Best for: People who expect to be in a LOWER tax bracket in retirement than they are now.

Example: High earners in their peak earning years who will have lower income in retirement.

📊 Reduces your taxable income TODAY = lower current tax bill

Roth (401k or IRA)

How it works: Contribute after-tax dollars now, withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free.

Best for: Young people who expect to be in a HIGHER tax bracket in retirement.

Example: Students and young workers in low tax brackets now with high earning potential later.

🔮 Pay taxes while in low bracket = tax-free growth for decades!

The 50/30/20 Budget Rule

10 / 20

A Framework for Saving

The 50/30/20 rule is a simple budgeting guideline that helps you allocate your after-tax income:

50% → Needs: Housing, utilities, food, transportation, insurance, minimum payments

30% → Wants: Entertainment, dining out, hobbies, subscriptions, upgrades

20% → Savings & Investing: Emergency fund, retirement contributions, extra debt payments

🎯 If you can put 20% toward savings/investing, you're building a strong financial foundation!

The Power of Employer Match

11 / 20

Free Money You Can't Ignore

Many employers offer to match your 401(k) contributions up to a certain percentage. This is essentially free money!

Example: Your employer matches 100% of contributions up to 4% of your salary.

If you earn $50,000 and contribute 4% ($2,000), your employer adds another $2,000 = $4,000 total!

That's an immediate 100% return on your investment—no other investment can guarantee that.

⚠️ Not contributing enough to get the full employer match is literally leaving free money on the table!

Time Horizons and Risk

12 / 20

Your Timeline Affects Your Strategy

How much time you have until retirement determines how much risk you can reasonably take with your investments.

  • 40+ Years (Age 20-25): Can be aggressive (80-90% stocks). Plenty of time to recover from market dips.
  • 25-40 Years (Age 25-40): Growth-focused with some stability. Gradually increase bonds (70-80% stocks).
  • 10-25 Years (Age 40-55): Balanced approach. Protect gains while still growing (60-70% stocks).
  • Under 10 Years (Age 55+): Conservative. Prioritize preserving what you've built (40-50% stocks).

🔄 Target-date funds automatically shift from aggressive to conservative as you approach retirement!

Real-World Example

Micah's Smart Start at 22

Micah gets his first job at 22, earning $45,000/year. His employer offers a 401(k) with 4% match.

Micah contributes 6% of his salary ($2,700/year) to his 401(k). His employer matches 4% ($1,800/year). Total: $4,500/year going into retirement.

Result: Even if Micah never gets a raise and contributes the same amount for 43 years until age 65, at 7% average returns his 401(k) would grow to over $1.1 million!

Jordan Waits Until 32
