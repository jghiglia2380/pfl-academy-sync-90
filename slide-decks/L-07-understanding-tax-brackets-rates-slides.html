Key Terms

4 / 20
Marginal Tax Rate

The rate at which your LAST dollar of income is taxed

This is the highest bracket rate that applies to any of your income

Example: If you're in the "22% bracket," only your income ABOVE $47,150 is taxed at 22%
Effective Tax Rate

The AVERAGE rate at which all your income is taxed

Total tax ÷ Total income = Your actual overall tax percentage

Example: $6,307 tax on $50,000 income = 12.6% effective rate (not 22%!)

Key Terms

5 / 20
Tax Avoidance

LEGAL strategies to minimize your tax liability

Using deductions, retirement contributions, and tax credits allowed by law

Examples: 401(k) contributions, itemizing deductions, timing income
Tax Evasion

ILLEGAL actions to avoid paying taxes owed

Hiding income, falsifying records, or failing to file returns—criminal offense

Consequences: Fines, penalties, and imprisonment

The Tax Bracket Myth

6 / 20

What Many People Believe (Wrong!)

"If I earn more and move into a higher tax bracket, all my income will be taxed at the higher rate, so I might take home LESS money."

How It Actually Works

Only the income ABOVE each bracket threshold is taxed at that bracket's rate. Your previous income stays at lower rates.

Earning more ALWAYS means keeping more!

Myth
"I'll lose money if I get a raise"
Truth
Only NEW dollars are taxed at higher rates
Always
More income = more take-home pay

Avoidance vs. Evasion

12 / 20

Tax Avoidance (Legal)

  • Contributing to 401(k) or IRA
  • Taking legitimate deductions
  • Timing income recognition
  • Choosing tax-efficient investments
  • Using tax credits you qualify for
  • Structuring business for tax efficiency
  • Charitable giving strategies

Tax Evasion (Illegal)

  • Hiding income from IRS
  • Falsifying deductions
  • Claiming fake dependents
  • Undeclared offshore accounts
  • Failing to file required returns
  • Paying employees in cash to avoid reporting
  • Inflating business expenses

Understanding his tax situation for financial planning

Jared earns $50,000 as a marketing coordinator. He's offered $5,000 in overtime work. Some coworkers say "it's not worth it—you'll just lose it to taxes." But Jared knows better!

His overtime is taxed at his marginal rate (22%), so he'd net about $3,900. That's $3,900 more than he had before—not a loss!

S

Sara & Spouse: Married Filing Jointly, $110,000

How filing status affects their tax situation

Sara and her spouse have a combined income of $110,000. Filing jointly gives them access to wider tax brackets—the 22% rate doesn't start until $94,300 (vs. $47,150 for single filers).

If Sara (earning $90K) filed separately while her spouse (earning $20K) filed separately, they'd pay MORE total tax. Filing jointly saves them money!
