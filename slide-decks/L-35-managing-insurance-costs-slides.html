Key Terms

4 / 20

Premium

The amount paid regularly to maintain insurance coverage—monthly, quarterly, or annually
Example: Paying $250/month for health insurance, regardless of whether you use it

Deductible

The amount you must pay out-of-pocket before your insurance coverage begins
Example: With a $1,000 deductible and $3,000 damage, you pay $1,000, insurance pays $2,000

Co-payment (Co-pay)

A fixed amount you pay for a specific service, regardless of total cost
Example: $25 co-pay for each doctor visit, paid at time of service

Co-insurance

A percentage of costs you share with the insurance company after meeting your deductible
Example: 80/20 means insurance pays 80%, you pay 20% after deductible

Key Terms

5 / 20

Out-of-Pocket Maximum

The most you'll pay for covered services in a policy period (usually a year)
Example: After paying $6,000 total, insurance covers 100% for the rest of the year

Stop-loss Point

The maximum out-of-pocket co-insurance amount before the insurer covers all additional costs
Example: Your protection against catastrophic healthcare expenses

Claim

A formal request to an insurance company for coverage or compensation for a covered loss
Example: Filing a claim after your car is damaged in an accident

Rider

An add-on to an insurance policy that provides additional coverage
Example: Adding flood coverage to a standard homeowner's policy

The Premium vs. Out-of-Pocket Trade-off

7 / 20

⬆️ Higher Premiums

  • Lower deductibles
  • Lower co-insurance percentage
  • Lower out-of-pocket costs when claims occur
  • More predictable costs
  • Better for frequent healthcare users

⬇️ Lower Premiums

  • Higher deductibles
  • Higher co-insurance percentage
  • Higher out-of-pocket costs when claims occur
  • Lower guaranteed monthly costs
  • Better for healthy, low-usage individuals

✅ Higher Deductible Makes Sense If:

  • You have substantial emergency savings
  • You rarely file claims
  • You want to minimize guaranteed premium costs
  • You can handle a larger one-time expense

⚠️ Lower Deductible Makes Sense If:

  • You have limited savings
  • You anticipate needing to use insurance
  • You prefer more predictable costs
  • A large expense would cause hardship

How Insurance Costs Work Together

1

Pay Your Premium

Regular monthly payment keeps your coverage active—you pay this whether you use insurance or not

2

Pay 100% Until Deductible Met

When you receive care, you pay the full cost until you've paid your annual deductible amount

3

Pay Co-insurance After Deductible

After meeting your deductible, you pay your percentage (e.g., 20%) of additional costs

4

Co-insurance Continues Until Out-of-Pocket Max

You keep paying your percentage until you reach your annual out-of-pocket maximum

5

Insurance Covers 100%

Once you hit your out-of-pocket max, insurance covers 100% of covered services for the rest of the year

Strategies for Managing Insurance Costs

9 / 20

Five Key Strategies

1. Choose the Right Deductible

Match your deductible to your emergency savings and risk tolerance. Higher deductible = lower premiums.

2. Use Tax-Advantaged Accounts

HSAs offer triple tax advantages: tax-deductible contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for medical expenses.

3. Stay In-Network

Using in-network providers can significantly reduce costs—out-of-network often means higher deductibles and co-insurance.

4. Shop Around and Compare

Get quotes from multiple insurers. Compare all cost components, not just premiums. Bundle policies for discounts.

Discounts and Health Savings Accounts

10 / 20

💰 Health Savings Account (HSA)

Available with high-deductible health plans

  • Tax-deductible contributions
  • Tax-free growth
  • Tax-free withdrawals for medical expenses
  • Funds roll over year to year
  • Triple tax advantage!

📋 Flexible Spending Account (FSA)

Set aside pre-tax dollars for medical expenses

  • Reduces taxable income
  • Use for medical expenses
  • Use-it-or-lose-it (some rollover)
  • Set contribution at enrollment
  • Available with any plan type

🏷️ Common Insurance Discounts

  • Safe driving record
  • Good student discount
  • Home security systems
  • Non-smoking status
  • Good credit score
  • Multi-policy bundle
  • Preventive wellness programs
  • Low mileage (auto)

Real-World Example: Sophia's Health Plan Decision

Comparing Two Health Insurance Plans

Sophia, 25, is healthy and rarely visits the doctor beyond annual check-ups. She's choosing between:

Plan A (Low-Deductible):

  • • $250/month premium ($3,000/year)
  • • $500 deductible
  • • $3,000 out-of-pocket max

Plan B (High-Deductible + HSA):

  • • $150/month premium ($1,800/year)
  • • $2,000 deductible
  • • $5,000 out-of-pocket max

✓ Sophia Chooses Plan B

Saves $1,200/year in premiums ($100/month × 12)

✓ Preventive Care Still Free

Annual check-ups covered 100% before deductible

💰 HSA Advantage

Can invest premium savings in tax-advantaged HSA for future medical needs

Key Takeaways

The Premium Trade-off

Higher premiums = lower out-of-pocket costs. Lower premiums = higher out-of-pocket costs. Balance based on your situation.

Know All Components

Premiums, deductibles, co-pays, co-insurance, and out-of-pocket maximums all affect your total costs.

Skill Builder: Calculate Your Out-of-Pocket Costs

Insurance Cost Calculation

Your health plan: $500 deductible, 20% co-insurance, $2,000 out-of-pocket max. You have an $8,000 medical bill.

Step-by-Step Calculation

1

How much is your deductible? → $500

2

How much remains after deductible? → $8,000 - $500 = $7,500

3

Calculate your 20% co-insurance → $7,500 × 0.20 = $1,500

4

Does total exceed the cap? → $500 + $1,500 = $2,000 (equals cap exactly!)

5

Your total out-of-pocket: $2,000 (Insurance pays $6,000)
